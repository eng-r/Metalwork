# Machining and Cutting Mechanics

## Treat this as machining, but preserve the unusual geometry

The tool is not a conventional 3-axis machine with a programmed Cartesian feed. Axial feed is generated by the hydraulic pusher, and the target may be a NiCr/nickel-alloy ball or other curved object.

Use machining theory for local chip formation and force generation, while computing engagement from the actual cutter-target geometry.

## Geometry layer

Represent:

- cutter radius and cylindrical envelope;
- tooth/flute count;
- cutting-edge orientation/helix if known;
- spindle angle;
- cutter axial position;
- target sphere/ball radius and center;
- current removed-material surface.

For an initial control-oriented model, analytic sphere/cylinder intersection or a local engagement-depth approximation may be enough.

For more complex target evolution, consider a signed-distance/voxel/height-field representation rather than a full finite-element model.

## Mechanistic force model

A useful baseline is the mechanistic milling model:

- tangential, radial and axial force contributions;
- shear/cutting coefficient times instantaneous uncut chip thickness;
- edge/ploughing coefficient term;
- integration/summation over engaged cutting-edge segments.

Compute spindle cutting torque from tangential force and local radius.

Compute axial reaction force needed by the pusher from the resolved cutting-force components and geometry.

## Two fidelity levels are required

### Averaged force/torque model

Average over tooth passage or spindle revolution. Use this for controller design, parameter sweeps and long simulations.

### Tooth-resolved model

Include discrete tooth engagement, runout/eccentricity and force ripple. Use this to test filters, observers and controller robustness.

## Material removal state

The target geometry must evolve as material is removed. Otherwise the model cannot reproduce the user's key behavior: reducing pusher pump RPM and waiting can allow cutting to remove material, reducing contact load/pressure.

Material removal rate should depend on local chip load and cutting kinematics, with guards against nonphysical removal when there is no cutting engagement.

## Nickel/NiCr material guidance

Do not treat "NiCr" as one unique material. Require actual grade/hardness if possible.

For nickel-based heat-resistant alloys, use manufacturer machining guidance only to establish plausible initial speed/feed ranges and constraints. Identify cutting-force coefficients experimentally for the actual tool/material/coolant condition.

## Cutting-process nonlinearities to consider

- entry/exit transients;
- minimum chip thickness / rubbing;
- tool runout;
- edge-force dominance at small chip loads;
- work hardening;
- wear/notching if experiment duration warrants it;
- chatter/structural vibration only if data indicate control relevance.

## Calibration tests

Use controlled cuts spanning:

- spindle speed;
- axial force/WOB or feed;
- engagement depth;
- material condition;
- tool condition.

Fit coefficients on one dataset and validate on held-out cutting conditions.
